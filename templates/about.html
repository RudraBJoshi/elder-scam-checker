How This Works

This tool reads the words in a message and compares them to patterns commonly found in scam messages that target seniors — things like urgent deadlines, requests for gift cards or wire transfers, secrecy, and impersonation of government agencies or tech companies.

It was trained on thousands of real examples of scam and legitimate messages, plus original examples grounded in the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) 2025 Annual Report on elder fraud.

Two ways we check

Every message gets checked two ways: a statistical model trained on thousands of real messages looks at word patterns and writing style, while a separate rule-based layer checks for specific tactics scammers use — urgency, secrecy, requests for gift cards, impersonation, and more. A message is only marked "high risk" when both agree there's real evidence, not just a raw score, so an unusual-but-harmless message doesn't cause a false alarm.

Scam patterns we recognize

  • Grandparent Scam
  • Government Impersonation
  • Tech Support Scam
  • Romance Scam
  • Investment Fraud
  • Gift Card / Favor Scam
  • Lottery / Sweepstakes Scam
  • Phishing / Fake Company Alert
  • Fake Package Delivery Notice
  • Fake Job or Business Opportunity

These match the patterns the San Diego County District Attorney's Office identified as most common in its November 2025 elder fraud prevention campaign: fake account alerts, law-enforcement impersonation, and fake tech-support warnings.

What it can't do

It is not perfect, and it can't call anyone to verify a message for you. Always trust your judgment — if a message asks for money, gift cards, or personal information and you weren't expecting it, call the person or organization directly using a phone number you already know is correct, not one from the message itself.
